Running the Game

This page is for whoever runs the world: a Narrator, or in Solo and Co-Op play the players themselves. The rules say what happens; this page says how to make it feel like the Bleak Forest.

Before Play: Session Zero and Safety

The Bleak Forest is a horror game. Before the first session (see Starting a Campaign), talk through what the game will contain and agree on how anyone can stop or steer it.

  • Name the content. The rules include death and Recursion, grief and apathy as Complications, possession and the Consumed, bodily Corruption, cults, the undead, and harm to the innocent. Ask what each person is happy to see, see only off-screen, or not see at all.
  • Lines and veils. A line is content that never appears. A veil is content that happens off-screen: the scene cuts away. Write them down and keep them where everyone can see.
  • A pause signal. Agree on a word, a gesture or a card (an X-card) that anyone can use, without explaining why, to skip or change what is happening. Honour it at once.
  • Check in. After a hard scene, and at the end of each session, ask how people are doing. In solo play, the same applies to you: stop when it stops being fun.
  • Agree on the table. How lethal should it be? Will you use Lean Play? How often will you meet, and what happens when someone misses a session?

Preparing a Session

  1. Read the notes from last time: where the characters are, which events are Prepared, where the Ruin die stands.
  2. Prepare the next Ordeal the characters are likely to reach: its place, its Fragments and where they lie, its encounters, and its Omen. The generators do most of this.
  3. Pick two or three people the characters may meet, with a want each (see People on the Fly).
  4. Choose one way the Adversary’s lie or a character’s Complication could turn up, so you are ready to offer trouble (see Doom and Boon).
  5. Stop there. The Danger Die and the players will supply the rest.

Running Ordeals

  • Pace. A Minor Ordeal fits in a session. A Moderate Ordeal takes two or three, and a Major Ordeal three to five. End a session on an Omen or a Setback, not in the middle of a fight.
  • Make clues fair. Put more than one way to every true Fragment: a Witness who talks, a Relic to find and a Mark to read. If the characters miss one, they reach the Fragment another way, at a cost such as time, a Setback or Doom. A Fragment is never lost for good.
  • Taint is the fair signal. Describe the Taint every time it appears, the same way each time. Players who notice it should feel clever, not lucky.
  • False Fragments must be beatable. Every False Fragment needs a tell the characters can find, by testing it or by noticing it does not fit (see Red Herrings).
  • Show the Ruin die. Keep it on the table. Lower it where everyone can see.

When to Roll, and When Not To

  • Roll only when failure would matter and the outcome is in doubt. If nothing interesting happens on a failure, the characters simply succeed, given time.
  • Say the stakes first. Tell the player what they risk before they roll: time, a Setback, Vitality, Doom or attention.
  • Failure moves the story. A failed check should change the situation, never stop it: the door opens but the guards hear, the trail is found but a day is lost.
  • Reward style. When a player Succeeds with style, give them something extra they can feel.

Playing the Adversary

  • The Adversary is patient and absent. It works through servants, lies and Holds, and is not met until the Reckoning. Let the players feel its hand before they learn its name.
  • The lie is the weapon. Each Goal has a favourite lie. Have its servants and fooled folk tell it, kindly and often, so that the truth costs something to believe.
  • Offer trouble. When a character’s Complication or the Adversary’s lie would bite, offer it: the player takes it for a Boon Point, or refuses. That exchange is the heart of the game.
  • Make Holds visible. When the Adversary gains a Hold, show it in the world: a new tower, a raised servant, a town gone quiet.

Splitting the Party

When characters are in different places, run them as separate groups.

  • Keep time in Watches. Resolve one Watch for each group in turn, then move to the next Watch. No group gets ahead of the others.
  • Each group rolls its own Danger Die for its own terrain and actions. Omen rolls are made for whichever group is inside the footprint.
  • Cut between groups at a cliffhanger. An encounter for one group plays out while the other group’s Watch waits.
  • Meeting again takes the travel it takes. Messages travel no faster than a rider.
  • Ordeals still need everyone: every character must be the Lead or a Second in at least one task (see Party Size).

Rewards and Advancement

Characters advance only by completing Ordeals and Training Ordeals (see Advancement). There is no reward for kills or treasure, by design: the road is the point. The other rewards are things the characters can hold: loot and coin, Standing, allies and hirelings, workings learned, Threads, and the places they have saved.
